My wallpaper is a slideshow of paintings. Caravaggio, Hokusai, Hopper, Klimt, Monet,
Vermeer, Beksiński; a few hundred of them, in no particular order. I love it, and it
made theming the rest of the desktop feel impossible. There is no colour scheme that
suits both a black Caravaggio and a white Sorolla beach.

I spent a while assuming I would have to trim the collection down to one mood.
This is about why I didn't, and what I built instead.

## `01` — The gallery

Museums hang wildly different work on the same wall. Same paint, same frames, same
lighting, same little labels, and it reads as one place. Nobody walks through the
Prado thinking the Goya clashes with the Bosch.

So the desktop became a gallery. The wallpaper is the artwork and changes whenever it
likes. Everything else is the wall: a fixed, museum-neutral chrome that never argues
with what is hanging on it. Cohesion comes from the frame, not from the paintings.

That decision settled the first half of the problem. The second half was choosing
the wall colour, and I didn't want to guess.

## `02` — Measuring the wall

I downscaled every image in the slideshow, converted it to a perceptual colour space,
and clustered the dominant colours. Aggregated over 477 paintings the collection is
not neutral at all. It is old varnish.

| measure | result |
| --- | --- |
| warm hues, umber and ochre through orange and yellow | 50% |
| neutrals | 30% |
| cool hues | 17% |
| median lightness | 43 / 100 |
| median saturation | 14 / 100 |

Half of everything on my wall is brown, most of the rest is grey, and almost none of
it is vivid. A warm, muted, dark base was not a taste decision. It was what the
paintings had already voted for.

## `03` — Pigments with provenance

A warm dark theme is a crowded field. Gruvbox lives there, and so do Everforest and
Cendre. My first draft was honestly just Gruvbox Material with a browner background;
the gold accent was the same hex value. It was fine. It was not mine.

What makes the themes I admire distinct is rarely their hues. Kanagawa is one
painting, named pigment by pigment. Cendre samples a wood fire and derives everything
from physics. Solarized Osaka keeps a classic palette but fixes it for modern
monitors and modern editor UIs. They each have a method and a story that survives a
screenshot.

I had a source none of them did: the wall itself. So every hue in Pinacoteca is
sampled from a specific painting in the slideshow, and the palette file records
which one.

<Palette title="pinacoteca · every hue names its painting" />

The wall and the linen both come from Vermeer's *Diana and Her Nymphs*, which
turned out to contain the entire umber ladder on its own. The red is the jester's
costume in Matejko's *Stańczyk*. The blue is the Prussian blue of *The Great Wave*.
The gold is a gold-ground portrait. Van Gogh supplied both the green and the aqua,
from two wheat fields painted a month apart.

Raw samples are far too dark for text, so the rule is: hue comes from the pigment,
lightness comes from a ladder. Colours are placed in OKLCH, every accent sits at
roughly the same perceived brightness, and every text colour clears 4.5:1 on the
background. Chroma is clamped into a band so the cool pigments stay quiet and the
warm ones stay warm.

## `04` — The rules

Six rules, in the spirit of Cendre's, so that future decisions are not taste calls.

1. **Every hue is sampled from a painting on the wall.** Nothing is invented, and
   the colours file says where each one came from.
2. **Hue from the pigment, lightness from the ladder.** Separation by lightness, not
   by hue. Minimum 4.5:1 for anything you read.
3. **The wall is neutral umber.** Surfaces differ by lightness only.
4. **One pigment per token family.** Strings are green, keywords are purple,
   functions are blue, types are gold. Identifiers and punctuation stay in linen.
   Colour marks meaning, not structure.
5. **Diagnostics are their own family.** An error is never the same highlight as a
   string, even when they share a hue.
6. **The frame responds, the text does not.** Borders, cursor and mode indicator may
   follow the current wallpaper. Syntax never does.

Rule six is the part no static theme can do, and it is the one I haven't wired up
yet. The base already holds on its own; the responsive frame is the next post.

## `05` — Where it lives

The source of truth is a single `colors.toml` in my dotfiles, laid out like an
Omarchy theme so it drops into one unchanged. Each tool then carries its own copy of
the numbers, because the tools run on Arch, NixOS and Windows and I wanted every
platform to link exactly the files it already linked.

<CodeExample file="theme/pinacoteca/colors.toml" note="excerpt">

```toml
accent = "#d7a447"
foreground = "#e6ccaf"
background = "#18130e"

[palette]
gold = "#d7a447"   # accent, types, warnings, search
blue = "#799dbb"   # functions, headings, info

[provenance.blue]
painting = "Katsushika Hokusai - Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave)"
sampled = ["#324c62"]
hue = 245
```

</CodeExample>

Ports so far: WezTerm, Alacritty, Ghostty, Windows Terminal, Neovim, Zed, VS Code,
tmux, Starship, fzf, btop, PSReadLine, and this site. The Neovim scheme is
generated from a base16 palette and then corrected for rules four and five. The
website you are reading swapped its tokens in one file.

<Callout type="tip" title="the collection stayed">
  Not one painting was removed for the sake of cohesion. Only exact duplicates and
  files too small for the monitor went. The Bosch is still in the rotation.
</Callout>
